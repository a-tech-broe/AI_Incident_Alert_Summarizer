I built an AI incident summarizer on AWS. The AI was the easiest part.

One file out of ten. None of the hard decisions.

The rest was the unglamorous work of making it trustworthy when its
dependencies misbehave — and that's what I wrote up. Eight real failures,
with the actual error text:

→ An OIDC trust policy correct in every single field, still denied by STS.
GitHub had quietly assigned the repo an immutable subject claim with numeric
IDs. Every name-based trust policy breaks and nothing tells you.

→ Every CloudWatch alarm silently dropped. Its `state` is an object, not a
string — so alarms parsed as "resolved" and vanished before any model call.
No error. No log line. A component reporting success while doing nothing.

→ `ThrottlingException: Too many tokens per day` that did not mean throttling.
The account was AUTHORIZED with a quota of zero. I got that diagnosis wrong
three times before I stopped reading the error and started querying the state.

Three design principles survived contact with reality:

• Enrichment never blocks notification. Six telemetry sources fan out under a
deadline that reserves 22 seconds for the model.

• Delivery is the last thing to fail. If Bedrock dies, the raw alert still
reaches Slack. A model outage must not become an alerting outage.

• Unavailable context is stated, not omitted. Drop a failed source silently and
the model can't tell "ECS was healthy" from "ECS was never queried" — absence
of evidence becomes evidence of absence.

Also in there: why confidence is high/medium/low and not 85%, why alert
payloads are untrusted input, and what it actually costs (~$3/month idle,
~$0.025 per alert).
